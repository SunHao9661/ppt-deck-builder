# Story System

Use this reference to turn source material into a presentation argument.

## Communication Contract

Record six decisions before outlining:

| Decision | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience | Who will receive this, and what do they already know? |
| Outcome | What should they understand, believe, choose, approve, or do? |
| Setting | Live room, recorded presentation, asynchronous read, or hybrid? |
| Reading mode | Presentation-led, balanced, or read-close? |
| Core message | What must survive if the audience remembers only one thing? |
| Afterlife | Will the deck be forwarded, audited, archived, or reused? |

The outcome determines the narrative; the setting determines density. A deck
that will be read without a presenter must carry more explanation than a live
keynote. Do not use one density for both.

## Source-Heavy or High-Stakes Work

For consulting, research, decision, or long source documents, do not jump from
notes to a single outline. First build an evidence layer:

| Evidence field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| claim/fact | What the source says |
| location | Page, section, table, timestamp, or URL |
| period/scope | Time range, cohort, region, sample |
| unit | Currency, percent, count, index, etc. |
| caveat | Missing condition or uncertainty |
| so-what | Why it matters to the audience outcome |
| visual candidate | Chart, table, matrix, timeline, or quote |

Keep source-backed and invented/demo values separate. Never let a generated
image or mock chart become evidence.

For large or ambiguous material, draft 2–3 possible storylines and compare:

- argument sharpness
- evidence strength
- decision relevance
- visual potential
- information density
- largest unresolved gap

Choose one recommended spine and state why the others were rejected. The user's
time should be spent choosing direction, not confirming a single arbitrary
outline.

## Choose One Narrative Spine

### SCR

Situation → Complication → Resolution. Strong for proposals, research,
diagnostics, and change stories.

### Pyramid / Answer First

Recommendation → reasons → evidence → implementation. Strong for executives,
investment committees, and time-constrained reviews.

### Funnel

Context → gap → approach → findings → implications. Strong for research,
technical reviews, and progressive explanation.

### Journey

Before → turning point → after → next move. Strong for case studies, customer
stories, retrospectives, and transformation narratives.

### Teach

Prerequisite → concept → worked example → practice/check → recap. Strong for
training, workshops, and courses.

Choose one spine for the deck. Section-level variation is allowed; narrative
drift is not.

## Build the Slide Roster

Use one line per slide:

```text
ID | page job | action title | evidence | caveat | so-what | visual form | source
```

Page jobs should be explicit:

- cover
- context
- problem/complication
- question
- method
- evidence
- comparison
- process/timeline
- implication
- decision/ask
- conclusion
- appendix

A page earns its place only if it changes audience understanding or the next
decision. Delete overview pages that merely preview the agenda and say nothing.

For high-density pages, plan the information zones before choosing a layout:

- one primary exhibit
- 2–4 supporting evidence blocks
- one caveat or limitation where needed
- one visible so-what/decision line
- compact source/footnote treatment

The layout must follow that information structure; do not fill a generic card
grid after the fact.

## Action Titles

Write a complete claim:

| Weak topic label | Strong action title |
|---|---|
| Market overview | Demand is shifting from broad reach to high-intent channels |
| Results | The new onboarding flow lifts activation by 18% |
| Risks | Three dependencies could delay launch beyond Q3 |
| Recommendation | Approve the pilot now; defer full rollout until security review |

Rules:

- Keep titles to one or two lines.
- Put the claim before the supporting details.
- Do not end titles with a period.
- Do not use generic labels such as “Overview,” “Discussion,” or “Next Steps”
  unless the page is genuinely navigational.
- Where the audience is neutral and technical, a precise descriptive title is
  acceptable; where a decision is required, use a decision-oriented title.

## Ghost-Deck Test

Read only the titles in order. The sequence should answer:

1. What is happening?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What evidence supports the claim?
4. What alternatives or risks remain?
5. What should happen next?

If a title can move elsewhere without changing the argument, it is weak or
unnecessary.

## Density and Slide Budget

Use time as a cue:

| Setting | Typical content pace |
|---|---|
| Live keynote / pitch | 1 claim per slide, sparse copy, strong visual |
| Business review | 1–2 related claims, concise evidence |
| Training / workshop | smaller steps, examples, checks, recaps |
| Async read-close | fuller assertions, annotations, sources, appendix |

For a live talk, 10–15 minutes usually needs a tight 8–12-slide core narrative,
not 30 pages. A 30-minute technical presentation may need 15–22 pages depending
on the number of diagrams. Slide count follows argument complexity, not a quota.

## Text Discipline

- A slide should communicate one primary claim.
- Prefer a short heading plus one explanatory line over a paragraph.
- Use bullets only for genuinely parallel or ordered items.
- Keep body text to a scan-friendly amount; when it no longer fits at a
  readable size, split, shorten, or move detail to notes/appendix.
- Use emphasis for orientation, not decoration.
- Never put presenter instructions in visible slide copy.

## Charts and Evidence

Every evidence page needs:

- a title stating what the evidence shows
- one primary exhibit
- a direct annotation or takeaway
- units, sample/date scope, and source
- enough context to avoid a misleading comparison

If two charts make two different points, use two pages. If they jointly make one
comparison, combine them only when the relationship is immediately clear.

Prefer charts for trends and comparisons, tables for precise lookup, and diagrams
for structure or process. Do not force every page to contain a chart.

## Speaker Notes

Write notes as delivery support:

- explain why the evidence matters
- add examples, caveats, and interpretation not visible on the slide
- give a natural transition to the next page
- use the user's spoken register and language

For a live talk, 100–250 Chinese characters or roughly 50–120 English words per
normal content slide is a useful range. Cover, section, and closing slides can be
shorter. Notes are not a transcript pasted under the slide.

## Ending

End with the decision, implication, next step, or conclusion the audience needs.
A contact-only “Thank you” slide is optional and must not displace the real
conclusion.
